## The short answer: where the official list is

The authority that publishes the UAE's treaties is the **Ministry of Finance**. It has two relevant pages: a general page on double taxation agreements, and an **International Treaties Dashboard** under open data. The dashboard describes itself as a complete list of treaties and agreements, with filters by treaty category and by country, and full-text PDFs. That is the list this guide refers to. We do not copy it here, for a good reason: a copied list goes out of date the day a treaty is signed, enters into force or is terminated.

If you are asking whether the UAE has a treaty with your country, open the dashboard, select your country and read the PDF. If you want to know what a treaty means for your own income, read on.

## Why the number of treaties differs from page to page

You will see "over 100", "137", "146" and "193" on different websites, and Ministry releases from different dates quote different totals. The Ministry of Finance's double taxation page itself gives two figures: it states that the UAE has concluded **137 double tax agreements** with most of its major trading partners, and also **193 double tax agreements and bilateral investment treaties** together. The second number includes agreements that protect investments, which are a different kind of treaty.

The practical lessons are three:

- **Never rely on one fixed figure** from a blog. The number changes.
- **A treaty signed is not always a treaty in force.** Check the status in the text or with the Ministry.
- **Investment treaties are not tax treaties.** They protect investors from non-commercial risks, they do not tell you which country taxes your salary.

The Ministry lists what the agreements aim to do: exempt or reduce taxes on income and profits, protect investments from non-commercial risks such as nationalisation, ensure the free transfer of profits in a freely convertible currency, and eliminate double taxation.

## What a double tax treaty actually does

A treaty is an agreement between two states. It does three things that matter to a person moving to Dubai.

1. **It defines residence and settles conflicts.** If both countries consider you resident, the treaty usually contains a tie-breaker based on where your permanent home is, where your personal and economic ties are strongest, where you habitually live and, if needed, your nationality. The order and wording are in the text of each treaty.
2. **It allocates taxing rights by type of income.** Salaries, business profits, dividends, interest, royalties, rental income, capital gains and pensions are usually dealt with in separate articles. A common pattern is that dividends and interest paid to a genuine resident of the other state are taxed mainly in the state of residence, while income from real estate remains taxable where the property is. The exact rule depends on the treaty.
3. **It reduces or removes withholding tax at source.** When a company or institution in your home country pays you dividends or interest, it may withhold tax. The treaty may lower the rate, if you can prove you are a resident of the UAE.

It does not do four things people often assume:

- It does not decide that you are no longer a resident of your home country. Your home country decides that under its own law.
- It does not apply automatically. You have to claim it, with documents.
- It does not remove corporate tax, VAT or social contributions in general.
- It does not cover every country. If your country is not in the dashboard, there is no treaty.

For a concrete case, read our article on the [France-UAE tax treaty](/en/blog/france-uae-tax-treaty/): it shows how a clause can let France keep taxing someone who remains tax-domiciled there, even when the person lives in Dubai.

## How to use a treaty in six steps

1. **Find the treaty.** Open the MoF dashboard, filter by your country and download the PDF. Check that it is in force, and look for an amending protocol.
2. **Establish your residence.** Count your days in the UAE, keep your Emirates ID, tenancy and employment or company documents. The UAE criteria are in our guide to the [UAE tax residency certificate](/en/uae-tax-residency-certificate/).
3. **Identify the income.** Treaty rules depend on the type of income: employment, dividends, interest, rent, capital gains, pensions. List what you will receive and where it comes from.
4. **Read the articles for that income.** Look for the definition of residence (usually Article 4), then the article for each type of income. Note any limitation clause, such as one that excludes people who remain domiciled at home.
5. **Obtain the proof the treaty requires.** For the UAE side, the Federal Tax Authority lists a tax residency certificate for the purposes of a double tax agreement. The treaty, or the foreign payer, may ask for a particular form and for the certificate to be issued for a specific period.
6. **Claim it before the payment**, not after. In many countries a payer applies the reduced rate only if it holds your proof at the time of payment. Getting a refund later is often slower.

## Common situations

### You are an employee in Dubai with no other income

The UAE does not tax personal salary income, so you will not pay tax on it in the UAE. The treaty matters only if your home country still considers you resident, or if you have income from home. See our overview of [tax in Dubai](/en/tax-in-dubai/).

### You hold shares or a rental property in your home country

Dividends and rent are the two types where a treaty is most often invoked. Rent from property located in your home country usually stays taxable there. Dividends may benefit from a reduced withholding rate if the treaty provides it and you give the paying institution your residence certificate in time.

### You own a UAE company and take dividends

The UAE side depends on the corporate tax regime and, for free zone companies, on qualifying status, covered in our article on [free zone companies and corporate tax](/en/blog/free-zone-corporate-tax-qfzp/). If you are a resident of a treaty country and the company is in the UAE, the treaty of your home country may decide whether it taxes the dividends you receive. Do not structure on the basis of a blog: ask a tax adviser who knows your home country.

### You are a remote employee of a foreign company

Here the home country and the employer's country may both claim to tax your salary. The treaty can help, but only if you are a UAE resident under the treaty's tests and your home country accepts it. We cover this in [remote worker tax and your home country](/en/blog/remote-worker-tax-home-country-dubai/).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Assuming the list proves you are protected.** A treaty has conditions, and a country in the list can still tax you.
- **Using the certificate of the wrong period.** The UAE certificate covers a past period of up to 12 months: ask for the period the other party names.
- **Forgetting that the other country's anti-avoidance rules apply.** Domestic law and treaty clauses can override a simple reading of the headline rate.
- **Taking a treaty rate from a blog.** Rates change with protocols and with multilateral instruments. Read the PDF.
- **Paying a fee for a list.** The official list is free, on the Ministry website.
