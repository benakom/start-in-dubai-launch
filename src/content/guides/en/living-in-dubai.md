## Living in Dubai: what to know before you go

Dubai attracts people because it combines several rare things in one place: safety, an economy open to foreigners, light personal taxation and a central position between Europe, Africa and Asia. But living in Dubai is not a long holiday. It is an expensive, demanding city where everything rests on your visa. This guide separates what is true from what belongs on a postcard.

If you are still at the planning stage, start with our guide to [moving to Dubai step by step](/en/moving-to-dubai/): it explains visas, documents and the order of the procedures.

## The advantages of living in Dubai

### A safe, well-organised city

Safety is the argument families mention most often. You can go out late at night, leave your phone on a café table, and children grow up in a very structured environment. Administrative procedures are largely digital (official apps, UAE PASS) and often quick.

### No income tax on salaries

The UAE does not levy income tax on salaries. Companies pay 9% tax on taxable profit above 375,000 AED, and VAT is 5% on most purchases. Be careful about your home country: depending on your situation, it may continue to tax you. See our guide to [tax in Dubai](/en/tax-in-dubai/).

### Career opportunities

Many international groups have their regional headquarters in Dubai. French is an asset in luxury, hospitality, real estate, finance and anything linked to francophone Africa and the Maghreb. Details are in our guide to [working in Dubai](/en/working-in-dubai/).

### A multicultural city

Expatriates are by far the majority and come from all over the world. Everyone keeps their own culture, language and festivals. Whether you come from Morocco, Tunisia, Senegal, France, Belgium, Canada or anywhere else, it is easy to find your community.

### A base for travelling

Dubai airport connects the city to Europe, the Maghreb, West Africa and Asia. Going home to see your family for a long weekend remains realistic from many cities.

## The disadvantages of living in Dubai

### The climate

From June to September temperatures very often exceed 40 °C, with high humidity on the coast. Life then moves between air-conditioned places: car, office, shopping mall. Winter (November to March), on the other hand, is very pleasant. If you struggle with heat, plan to travel in summer.

### The cost of living

Housing and private schools weigh heavily. Rent can absorb a large share of a salary, and schooling a child at a French school costs several tens of thousands of dirhams a year. The absence of income tax does not always make up for it. Orders of magnitude are in our guide to the [cost of living in Dubai](/en/cost-of-living-dubai/).

### A residence that is always tied to a visa

Your right to stay depends on your employer, your company or your investment. Losing your job means finding a new sponsor within a limited time. There is no unemployment insurance comparable to Europe's for expatriates, and no public social security: health insurance comes through your employer or from you. The Golden Visa brings more stability, see our guide to the [residence visa in Dubai](/en/residence-visa-dubai/).

### Distance

The time difference with Europe and the Maghreb is generally 2 to 3 hours depending on the season (Dubai does not change its clocks), slightly more with Morocco during Ramadan and with West Africa. It is much larger with Canada. Family celebrations, births, ageing parents: the distance is felt more than expected, especially in the second year.

### A fast-moving city

Dubai values visible success. The pace of work can be intense, and social life expensive if you follow the crowd. Many newcomers spend more than planned in their first year.

## Local rules to know

Dubai is tolerant towards foreigners, but it is a Muslim country with its own laws. Most rules are common sense:

- **Dress:** relaxed in general, more covered in traditional public places, government offices and mosques.
- **Alcohol:** consumed only in licensed venues (hotels, licensed restaurants) and at home. Never in public, and zero tolerance when driving.
- **Drugs:** very strict legislation, including for some substances that are legal elsewhere. Also check your prescription medicines before you travel.
- **Behaviour in public:** discretion with displays of affection, and no photos of people without their consent.
- **Social media:** insults, defamation and offensive posts online can have criminal consequences.
- **Ramadan:** discretion in public during the day, and working hours are often reduced.

## Living in Dubai as a couple

### Married couple

If you are married, the spouse who holds the visa can sponsor the other, provided they earn at least 4,000 AED a month, or 3,000 AED with accommodation provided (u.ae). You need a marriage certificate attested in your country and then in the UAE. The sponsored spouse can then work with a work permit obtained by their own employer.

### Unmarried couple

Federal criminal law reforms adopted since 2020 have relaxed the rules on living together outside marriage, but some situations remain regulated (a pregnancy outside marriage, for example): talk to a local lawyer if your situation requires it. In every case, an unmarried partner cannot be sponsored: each person must have their own visa (employment, company, freelance, Golden Visa and so on). Think about this before you leave, especially if one of you has no professional project on the ground yet.

### Living in Dubai with a family

With children, everything revolves around school, neighbourhood and health insurance. Our guide to [moving to Dubai with your family](/en/moving-to-dubai-with-family/) details children's visas, family-friendly areas and the school calendar.

## Daily life in Dubai

### Finding a home

Almost everyone rents at first, with an annual lease registered through **Ejari** at the Dubai Land Department. This number is required for electricity and water (DEWA), internet and often for family visas.

### Getting around

The metro, tram and buses run on the Nol card. The network covers the city's main axis well, and some residential areas less well. Most residents end up with a car; urban tolls (Salik) and parking add to the budget. Taxis and ride-hailing apps are plentiful.

### Shopping

You can find everything: international supermarkets, neighbourhood grocers, and French, North African, African and Asian products. Prices vary a lot between local or regional products and imported ones.

### Healthcare

Health insurance is mandatory. Employers insure their employees and the sponsor insures their family. Private clinics are numerous and French-speaking doctors exist, but check that they are in your insurance network.

### Everyday paperwork

The Emirates ID is your local identity card: bank, phone, rental, healthcare, everything goes through it. Keep it up to date, along with your visa. For a bank account, see our guide to [opening a bank account in Dubai](/en/open-bank-account-dubai/).

## International communities in Dubai

Expat communities are large and well organised, and French speakers in particular are numerous:

- **Consular offices:** France, Morocco, Belgium, Switzerland, Canada and many African countries have an embassy in Abu Dhabi and often a consulate in Dubai, useful for civil status documents, passports and powers of attorney.
- **Schools:** several accredited French lycées welcome students of all nationalities. See our guide to [French schools in Dubai](/en/french-schools-dubai/).
- **Culture and networking:** welcome associations for newcomers, entrepreneur networks, chambers of commerce, and WhatsApp and Facebook groups by country or neighbourhood.
- **Shops and services:** French-speaking restaurants, bakeries, doctors, lawyers and estate agents.

This network helps a great deal in the first months, but stay critical of the unverified advice that circulates in groups: for a visa, a tax rule or a lease, always refer to the official source.

## Before you decide: 7 questions to ask yourself

1. Do I have a clear residence route (employment, company, investment, studies)?
2. Does my budget work with an annual rent and, if needed, private school fees?
3. Do I have enough to cover three to six months of expenses in case of the unexpected?
4. Does my spouse also have a project on the ground?
5. Are my documents (diplomas, marriage and birth certificates) attested?
6. Have I checked what my home country still asks of me (tax, social protection, pension)?
7. Have I spent a few days on the ground, ideally in summer, to see whether this pace suits me?
