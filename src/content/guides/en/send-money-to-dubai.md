## What "sending money to Dubai" really involves

Four things happen when money travels: your bank or provider debits you, converts the currency, sends the payment through a network, and the UAE bank credits the beneficiary. Each step can cost money, and each step is regulated. This guide covers the legal ways to do it, how to compare the true cost, which documents banks expect, and the rule on carrying cash. It also covers the reverse direction, sending money out of Dubai, because most people who move do both.

If you do not yet have a UAE account, read our guide to [opening a bank account in Dubai](/en/open-bank-account-dubai/) and the comparison of [how to choose a bank in Dubai](/en/best-banks-dubai-expats/) first, because the account decides how you receive money.

## The legal channels: banks and licensed exchange houses

The Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE) regulates who may move money. Its exchange business regulation states that nobody may carry on exchange business in the UAE unless licensed by the Central Bank, and that banks licensed under the Central Bank Law are treated as licensed to carry on exchange business. In practice, that gives you two legal channels in the UAE:

- **A bank**, from your own account or the beneficiary's.
- **A licensed exchange house or remittance provider**, which can send and receive money for a customer, often with cash collection or with digital transfer.

How to check: before you use a provider that is not a bank, confirm on the Central Bank's website that it is licensed. Do not rely on a shop sign, a social media page or a recommendation in a chat group.

What to avoid: informal "hawala-style" arrangements and unlicensed transfer agents. Money sent that way has no regulated trail, may expose you to freezing and loss, and can cause problems with the bank later.

## Use the IBAN exactly as written

Since 2012, u.ae states, an IBAN has been mandatory for electronic payments and receipts inside and outside the UAE (credit card payments excepted). Each account has its own IBAN, and the bank must tell the account holder theirs. For you, that means:

1. Ask the beneficiary for the IBAN, the bank name, the account name and, for international payments, the SWIFT code.
2. Copy them exactly. One wrong digit can delay or return the payment.
3. Send to an account **in the beneficiary's own name**. A payment to a third party raises questions and can be blocked.
4. Keep the transfer confirmation. You can need it for the bank's source of funds questions or a tax file.

## The true cost: fee, rate and speed

Most people compare the fee. The cost that matters is the **amount that arrives**. Compare three items.

| Item | What to ask | Where the hidden cost sits |
|---|---|---|
| Fee | A fixed or percentage fee charged by the sender | Easy to see |
| Exchange rate | The rate applied against the market rate on the same day | The gap (the margin) is often larger than the fee |
| Intermediary and receiving fees | Whether correspondent banks or the beneficiary's bank deduct anything | Can reduce the amount that arrives |

A simple test, using a made-up example for illustration only: if the market rate lets 1,000 units of your currency buy 4,000 AED, and a provider offers you 3,920 AED with a zero fee, the "free" transfer cost you 80 AED. Another provider offering 3,970 AED with a 20-unit fee may be cheaper. Compare the final amount received for the same amount sent, at the same moment, and write down the time.

Speed also has a cost. A payment that arrives late can cost you a rent deposit, a visa-related payment or a fee for late payment. If a date matters, ask the provider for the expected delivery and what happens if it is delayed.

## Documents banks may ask for

Licensed institutions in the UAE run customer due diligence under Central Bank rules, so questions about a transfer are normal. Keep these ready, in your own name:

- passport, and the Emirates ID and residence visa if you live in the UAE;
- proof of address;
- proof of the **source of funds** for a large amount: payslips, a sale contract, a dividend voucher, a tax return, a gift deed or an inheritance document;
- the reason for the transfer: rent, school fees, purchase, family support, investment.

A clean file reduces delays. If you are asked for a document that you do not have, say so early: improvising explanations is more damaging than a short delay.

## Carrying cash: the AED 60,000 rule

You can carry cash, but the amount has to be declared above a threshold. The ICP states that travellers entering or leaving the UAE with more than **AED 60,000**, or the equivalent in other currencies, negotiable financial instruments, precious metals or stones, must disclose it through the **Afseh** system, on the website or in the app, before or during the trip. The authority says disclosure carries no taxes or fees. Each family member aged 18 or over can carry that amount without declaring it, and amounts carried by anyone under 18 are added to the accompanying adult's total.

Penalties for not declaring can include a customs fine and, depending on the case, referral to law enforcement. Do not split cash among travellers to stay just under the threshold: that is the kind of behaviour that attracts questions. The simple, safe approach is to declare.

Remember that the other country has its own rules: your departure airport can have a different threshold.

## Sending money out of the UAE to your home country

This is the reverse path and it works with the same two legal channels. What changes is the receiving side.

- **Check the home country's rules** for incoming transfers: some require a purpose code, a form or a declaration, and some have limits. Ask your local bank, or read the page of your central bank or exchange control authority.
- **Keep the proof of origin.** A salary transfer from a UAE account is easy to explain; savings built up over years are easier to explain if you have the statements.
- **Plan for tax.** The UAE does not tax salaries, but your home country may tax what it sees as income of a resident. See our guide on [tax in Dubai](/en/tax-in-dubai/) and, if you run a company, our guide to [company formation in Dubai](/en/company-formation-dubai/).

For specific rules from Morocco or Algeria, including exchange control for a company owner, see our guides to [business setup for Moroccans](/en/business-setup-dubai-for-moroccans/) and [for Algerians](/en/business-setup-dubai-for-algerians/).

## Sending money for a company

A company account in the UAE is not opened the same way as a personal account, and the bank looks at the activity, the owners and the origin of funds. Paying in share capital or sending money to a company account needs the contract, the invoice or the shareholder resolution that explains the payment. Read our guides on [non-resident business bank accounts](/en/business-bank-account-dubai-non-resident/) and the [reasons a UAE bank rejects a company account](/en/blog/uae-bank-account-rejected-reasons/) before you wire anything.

## If something goes wrong

1. **Contact the sender's bank or provider immediately** with the transfer reference.
2. **Contact the receiving bank** with the same reference and ask whether the funds arrived or were returned.
3. **Complain in writing** to the institution. If the answer does not solve it, u.ae points to Sanadak, the government platform for banking-related complaints.
4. **Never pay a fee to "unlock" a transfer.** That is a common scam.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Comparing fees and ignoring the rate.**
- **Sending to a third party's account** or to an account that is not in the beneficiary's name.
- **Using an unlicensed agent** because the rate looks better.
- **Carrying more than AED 60,000 without declaring it.**
- **Sending a large sum with no documents to prove its origin.**
- **Waiting until the last day** for a deadline that depends on the transfer.
