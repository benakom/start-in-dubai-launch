## Why move to Dubai?

Every year, thousands of people from around the world choose to relocate to Dubai. The reasons that come up most often are simple:

- **Personal taxation.** The UAE does not levy income tax on salaries. Companies pay a 9% tax on taxable profit above 375,000 AED, and VAT is 5%.
- **A market open to foreigners.** A large majority of residents are expatriates. You can set up a business, work, invest and send your children to school in a system designed for foreign residents.
- **Location.** Dubai is a few hours by plane from Europe, North Africa, Africa and Asia, which makes it an ideal base for a business serving several markets.
- **Quality of life.** Modern infrastructure, fast services, largely digital procedures, French and international schools.

You should also look at the downsides: a high cost of living (especially housing and schools), a very hot summer, no public social security for expatriates, and a right to stay that always depends on a visa. We cover them in our guide to [living in Dubai, pros and cons](/en/living-in-dubai/).

## The 4 routes to residency in the UAE

To live in Dubai you must hold a residence visa. It is attached to a "sponsor": your employer, your company, yourself in some cases, a family member or your university. Before you move to Dubai, choose your route: everything else follows from it.

| Route | Who sponsors you | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Employment | Your employer | Employees hired by a local or free zone company |
| Company or freelance | Your company, or yourself with a freelance permit | Entrepreneurs, self-employed people, consultants |
| Investment (Golden Visa, Green Visa) | Yourself | Property or business investors, qualified profiles |
| Studies | The university or a resident parent | Students enrolled in an approved institution |

### 1. Through a job

This is the most common route. The employer applies for your work permit (with the Ministry of Human Resources, MOHRE, or the authority of its free zone), then for your entry permit and residence visa. Recruitment and visa costs are paid by the employer. Everything is explained in our guide to [working in Dubai](/en/working-in-dubai/).

### 2. Through your own company or freelance status

By setting up a company, in a free zone or on the mainland, you obtain an investor or partner visa, and you can then sponsor employees and your family. The steps, the types of structure and the pitfalls are detailed in our guide to [company formation in Dubai](/en/company-formation-dubai/) and in our comparison of [free zones in Dubai](/en/free-zone-dubai/). If you work alone, also look at [freelance status in Dubai](/en/freelance-visa-dubai/): the MOHRE freelance permit is one of the few permits you apply for yourself.

Be careful: an [offshore company in Dubai](/en/offshore-company-dubai/) does not give you a residence visa. This is a common confusion.

### 3. Through an investment: Golden Visa and Green Visa

The **Golden Visa** is a long residence of 5 or 10 years depending on the category, renewable, which does not depend on any employer and lets you sponsor your spouse and children. It is open in particular to investors (including property investors from 2 million AED), entrepreneurs, talents and qualified profiles, and top students. For property, official sources do not all say the same thing: the federal portal u.ae states 5 years, while the ICP and the GDRFA communicate 10 years. Rely on the authority that issues the visa (the GDRFA in Dubai, the ICP in the other emirates). The precise conditions, category by category, are in our guide to the [Golden Visa in Dubai](/en/golden-visa-dubai/).

The **Green Visa** (5 years) is aimed at skilled employees (salary of at least 15,000 AED a month according to the official portal), freelancers, and investors or business partners. For an overview of all residence permits, see our guide to the [residence visa in Dubai](/en/residence-visa-dubai/). And if you are thinking of investing more broadly, our guide to [investing in Dubai](/en/invest-in-dubai/) reviews the options.

### 4. Through studies

A student enrolled in an approved university can be sponsored by the institution or by a resident parent. Our guides to [studying in Dubai](/en/study-in-dubai/) and the [student visa in Dubai](/en/student-visa-dubai/) detail the procedure.

### Other cases: family, remote work, retirement

- **Family:** a resident can sponsor their spouse and children if they earn at least 4,000 AED a month, or 3,000 AED with accommodation provided (u.ae). Sons can be sponsored up to age 25, daughters as long as they are unmarried.
- **Remote work:** if you work remotely for a company based outside the UAE, a one-year remote work visa exists, with a minimum monthly income of 3,500 US dollars according to the [GDRFA Dubai](https://www.gdrfad.gov.ae/en/node/13898).
- **Retirement:** a 5-year retirement visa exists from age 55, after at least 15 years of career and subject to income or wealth conditions ([u.ae](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visa-and-emirates-id/Types-of-visas/Residence-visa/residence-visa-for-the-retired)). The thresholds are detailed in our guide to the [residence visa in Dubai](/en/residence-visa-dubai/).

## How to move to Dubai: the steps in order

The same steps come up in almost every case. The order matters: without a visa, no Emirates ID; without an Emirates ID, in most cases no Ejari tenancy registration and no resident bank account.

### 6 months before departure

- Choose your residence route (job, company, investment, studies).
- Draw up a realistic budget: rent, deposit, schools, insurance, a few months of expenses in advance.
- Gather your documents and have them legalised (see the next section). This is often the longest step.
- If you have children, contact schools now: places in French schools are limited. See our guide to [French schools in Dubai](/en/french-schools-dubai/).
- Find out about your tax and social security obligations in your country of departure.

### 3 months before

- Start your job search, or the creation of your company.
- If possible, make a scouting trip: neighbourhoods, commutes, schools.
- Check the validity of your passport (allow a margin of several months).
- Prepare the move: what you take, what you sell.

### 1 month before

- Book temporary accommodation (furnished apartment, serviced residence) for the first weeks.
- Arrange health cover for the transition: travel or international cover until you get local insurance.
- Notify your bank, your insurers and the authorities in your home country.

### The first weeks in Dubai

1. **Entry with the entry permit** issued by your sponsor, or a change of status inside the country if that is possible in your case.
2. **Medical test** in an approved centre, mandatory from age 18.
3. **Emirates ID:** biometric fingerprints, then issue of the card.
4. **Residence visa** applied electronically to your file.
5. **Bank account:** see our guide to [opening a bank account in Dubai](/en/open-bank-account-dubai/).
6. **Final tenancy contract and Ejari**, then connection of electricity and water (DEWA) and internet.
7. **Health insurance** for you and your dependants.
8. **Driving licence:** depending on your country, it may be converted or you have to take the local test (list on the RTA website).

## Documents to legalise before you leave

The UAE only accepts a foreign document (degree, marriage certificate, birth certificate) if it has been legalised. It is not a party to the Hague Apostille Convention ([official HCCH status table](https://www.hcch.net/en/instruments/conventions/status-table/?cid=41)), so an apostille alone is not enough. The usual procedure, described by the UAE Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MOFA), goes through several stamps:

1. Certification by the authorities of your country (often the Ministry of Foreign Affairs or the authority that issued the document).
2. Legalisation by the UAE embassy or consulate in the country where the document was issued.
3. Final legalisation by the MOFA, once in the UAE or through its online services.
4. Legal translation into Arabic when the administration requires it.

| Document | What it is used for |
|---|---|
| Highest degree | Work permit for some positions, Golden Visa or Green Visa for qualified profiles |
| Marriage certificate | Sponsoring your spouse |
| Children's birth certificates | Sponsoring your children, school enrolment |
| School certificates, report cards | School enrolment and equivalences |
| Bank statements, proof of income | Depending on the visa (remote work, Golden Visa, family sponsorship) |

Requirements and the exact order of stamps vary according to the country of issue and the visa: ask the UAE embassy or consulate in your country before starting the procedure. The steps specific to each nationality are in our country guides: [from Morocco](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-morocco/), [from Algeria](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-algeria/), [from Tunisia](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-tunisia/), [from Senegal](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-senegal/), [from France](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-france/), [from Belgium](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-belgium/), [from Switzerland](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-switzerland/) and [from Canada](/en/moving-to-dubai-from-canada/).

## Housing in Dubai: renting, Ejari and buying

### Renting

Most newcomers rent first. Leases are usually annual and rent is often paid in several cheques (from 1 to 4, sometimes more), with a deposit and, if you go through an agency, a commission. Prices vary enormously from one neighbourhood to another: a studio in a remote area has nothing in common with a two-bedroom flat in the city centre.

### Registering the tenancy contract: Ejari

Every tenancy contract in Dubai must be registered with the Dubai Land Department through the **Ejari** system. This number is required to connect electricity and water (DEWA), internet and, in many cases, for your family's visas. Registration is done online (Dubai REST app, Dubai Now) or in an approved centre, and must be renewed each time the lease is renewed.

### Buying

If you are considering buying, to live in or to invest, that is a different subject, with its own rules and its own risks. For property, START IN DUBAI works with [Cevitas Real Estate](https://www.cevitas.ae), a RERA-licensed agency (ORN 49294).

## Opening a bank account

With your Emirates ID and your visa, opening a current account is usually quick. Some banks accept non-residents, on stricter conditions. For a company, banks examine the activity, the origin of funds and the real presence in the UAE: allow several weeks. Comparison and documents are in our guide to [opening a bank account in Dubai](/en/open-bank-account-dubai/), and the tax rules in our guide to [taxes in Dubai](/en/tax-in-dubai/).

## Health and health insurance

In Dubai, health insurance is mandatory. The employer must insure its employees, and the sponsor must insure the people they sponsor (spouse, children, domestic workers). Since 1 January 2025, health insurance is also required for private sector employees in the other emirates, when the visa is issued or renewed.

The healthcare system combines public hospitals and a large network of private clinics. The extent of cover varies a lot from one policy to another (maternity, dental, clinic network, cover abroad): read the table of benefits before signing, especially if your employer does not cover your family.

## Schools: start early

Private schools charge fees, and these are regulated by the Dubai education authority (KHDA). Accredited French schools are in high demand: waiting lists, entrance tests, files to prepare months in advance. In French schools, tuition fees start at about 30,000 AED a year in nursery and often exceed 60,000 AED in the final years of secondary school, depending on the school and the level, excluding registration fees, uniform and transport (each school publishes its KHDA-approved fee schedule). For the 2026-2027 school year, the KHDA announced that private school fees in Dubai would not increase.

Before signing a lease, check the travel time between home and school at rush hour: it is often the school that decides the neighbourhood, not the other way round. Also think about the documents required at enrolment (report cards, leaving certificate from the previous school, vaccination record), to be prepared before departure. Everything is detailed in our guides to [moving to Dubai with your family](/en/moving-to-dubai-with-family/) and [French schools in Dubai](/en/french-schools-dubai/).

## Cost of living: orders of magnitude

Housing and schools make the difference between a comfortable budget and a tight one. There is no official statistic by profile, but according to our estimates a single person spends in the order of 8,000 to 15,000 AED a month, a couple between 12,000 and 22,000 AED, and a family with two children in private school between 25,000 and 45,000 AED, or even more. These ranges depend mainly on the neighbourhood and the type of housing. In euros, on the indicative basis of 1 euro for about 4.3 AED, that is roughly 1,900 to 3,500 euros for a single person, 2,800 to 5,100 euros for a couple and 5,800 to 10,500 euros for a family. The dirham is pegged to the US dollar: its rate against the euro varies, so recalculate at the day's rate.

What your employer covers changes everything: a housing allowance, family health insurance or a contribution to school fees can represent several thousand dirhams a month. The item-by-item detail is in our guide to the [cost of living in Dubai](/en/cost-of-living-dubai/).

## Dubai or another emirate?

The United Arab Emirates has seven emirates. Visa rules are federal, but each emirate has its own authorities, its rents and some local rules.

- **Dubai:** the economic centre, most free zones and regional headquarters, the densest social life.
- **Abu Dhabi:** the capital, quieter, with its own free zones and a major role in government and energy.
- **Sharjah and Ajman:** often lower rents, but sometimes long commutes to Dubai at rush hour, and stricter local rules in Sharjah.
- **Ras Al Khaimah, Fujairah, Umm Al Quwain:** further away, with free zones that are sometimes cheaper for setting up a company.

Your company can be registered in one emirate and you can live in another: just check the consequences for your visa and your lease.

## Mistakes to avoid when moving to Dubai

- **Arriving on a tourist visa to "look around on the spot" without a plan.** It is possible for a scouting trip, but working on a visit visa is forbidden.
- **Forgetting to legalise documents.** A marriage certificate that is not legalised blocks your spouse's visa.
- **Signing a lease before you have your visa.** Without an Emirates ID, Ejari and utility connections become complicated.
- **Underestimating the starting budget.** Deposit, agency commission, several rent cheques, furniture, car: the first months are expensive.
- **Choosing a company structure without thinking about the visa or the bank.** A cheap structure that does not let you open a bank account is not a saving.
- **Believing you will no longer pay any tax.** Your home country may keep taxing rights if you remain tax resident there. See our guide to [taxes in Dubai](/en/tax-in-dubai/).
- **Registering with schools too late.** Places go quickly, especially for the September intake.
