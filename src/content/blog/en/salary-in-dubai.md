## Salary in Dubai: what to know before comparing

In Dubai, you do not compare salaries, you compare **packages**. Two offers showing the same amount can give very different standards of living depending on whether the employer provides housing, insures the family, pays for school or reimburses a flight a year.

Three basic rules, all set by official texts:

- **no income tax** on salaries in the UAE;
- **no general minimum wage** for private-sector expatriates: according to u.ae, the labour law does not provide for one, and the amount is set in the contract;
- **an end-of-service gratuity** due after one year, calculated on base salary only.

If you are still looking for a job, start with our guide to [working in Dubai](/en/working-in-dubai/) (search, work permit, contract, scams). This article focuses on a single question: is the offer you received a good one?

## Average salary in Dubai: why the figures going around mislead

The search "average salary in Dubai" returns very different figures from one site to another. That is no accident:

- **There is no official pay scale by profession.** Public employment surveys (Dubai Statistics Center, the federal statistics centre) mostly describe the working population and salary bands, not the pay of an accountant or a nurse.
- **Averages mix very different worlds.** Dubai's workforce runs from construction workers to bank executives. An average across everyone says nothing about your role.
- **Salary sites rely on declarations or published offers**, with varying methods, and sometimes confuse base salary and package.
- **The company's nationality and the sector count** as much as the job: an international bank, a local SME and a free zone do not pay the same way.

Our advice: do not use an "average salary" to decide. Use real offers for the same role, the view of recruiters who place this profile in Dubai, and above all the calculation of your disposable income (see the method below).

## Reading an offer: base salary, allowances, benefits

A job offer in Dubai almost always comes in several lines. The labour law distinguishes **base salary** from **total salary** (base plus allowances), and this difference has real effects.

| Offer element | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Base salary | The fixed part of the pay | Used to calculate end of service and overtime |
| Housing allowance | Monthly amount or housing provided | Often the heaviest item in the budget: compare it with real rents |
| Transport allowance | Monthly amount or vehicle | Useful if you do not live near the metro |
| Other allowances | Phone, education, etc. | Read whether they are fixed or conditional |
| Employee health insurance | Compulsory, paid by the employer | Check the level of cover, not just its existence |
| Family insurance | Not always included in Dubai | Can amount to several thousand dirhams a year |
| School fees | Fully, partly or not covered | Decisive for a family |
| Annual flight | Return trip to your home country | Common, to be specified (you alone or the whole family) |
| Bonus | Variable, often discretionary | Do not count it as guaranteed income |

**The most common trap**: an offer with a low base salary and large allowances. The total looks attractive, but your end-of-service gratuity and your overtime will be calculated on the base only. According to u.ae, overtime is paid at the normal hourly rate plus 25% (50% between 10 pm and 4 am), **calculated on the base salary**.

Insist that the offer and the contract state the base salary and each allowance separately, in figures.

## What is deducted from your salary (almost nothing)

### No income tax

The UAE does not levy income tax on individuals: your salary is not taxed there. The details (VAT, corporate tax, tax residence) are in our guide to [tax in Dubai](/en/tax-in-dubai/).

### Unemployment insurance, the only regular deduction

Since 2023, private-sector and federal-sector employees must subscribe to the job loss insurance (ILOE). According to u.ae:

- the contribution is **at most 5 AED a month** if your base salary is below 16,000 AED, **at most 10 AED** above that;
- in case of job loss (excluding resignation and disciplinary dismissal), after at least 12 months of contributions, it pays **60% of salary** for a maximum of 3 months, capped at 10,000 or 20,000 AED a month depending on the category;
- not subscribing, or not paying, exposes you to a 400 AED fine.

Investors who own their business, domestic workers and temporary contracts are excluded.

### No pension contribution for most expatriates

The UAE public pension scheme (GPSSA) is aimed at Emiratis and, through an extension programme, nationals of the Gulf countries. For other expatriates, there is no pension contribution on salary: the end-of-service gratuity plays that role, and preparing your retirement is up to you.

### How salary is paid

According to u.ae, private-sector salaries go through the **Wage Protection System (WPS)**, via a bank or an exchange house approved by the Central Bank. The salary for the month just ended is due on the first day of the following month. It is paid in dirhams, or in another currency if the contract provides for it. To receive your salary you will need a local account: see our guide to [opening a bank account in Dubai](/en/open-bank-account-dubai/).

## End-of-service gratuity: a lump sum paid at the end

This is the part most often forgotten in a salary in Dubai. According to u.ae, a full-time employee with at least one year of continuous service is entitled to:

- **21 days of base salary per year** for the first 5 years;
- **30 days of base salary per year** beyond 5 years;
- within a limit of **2 years of salary** in total.

It is calculated on the **last base salary**, excluding allowances, and the employer must pay it within 14 days of the end of the contract. It is prorated for part-time work.

As an order of magnitude, 21 days is about 0.7 months of base salary per year worked. On an offer, this amounts to adding a little over half a month of base pay a year to your remuneration, paid at the end.

Two variants exist:

- **The alternative savings scheme**: a private-sector or free-zone employer can choose to pay 5.83% of base salary each month (8.33% after 5 years) into an approved fund, instead of the standard gratuity. The employee can add voluntary contributions.
- **The DIFC**, Dubai's financial centre, has its own employment law and a mandatory savings plan (DEWS) that replaces the standard gratuity for employees working there.

Ask your future employer which of the three systems applies.

## The official salary thresholds that change your life

There is no pay scale, but the administration uses thresholds. They tell you what a salary allows you to do, not whether it is "good".

| Monthly threshold | What it is for | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 4,000 AED, or 3,000 AED with housing provided | Sponsoring your spouse and children | u.ae |
| 15,000 AED | 5-year Green Visa for a skilled employee (with at least a bachelor's degree) | ICP |
| 16,000 AED base salary | Unemployment contribution moves from 5 to 10 AED | u.ae |
| 30,000 AED | Skilled-profile Golden Visa (base or total: to be confirmed with the ICP or GDRFA) | ICP |

The Golden Visa and its other routes are explained in our guide to the [Golden Visa in Dubai](/en/golden-visa-dubai/).

## The 5-step method to evaluate an offer

### 1. Bring the whole package down to a monthly amount

Add the base salary and the fixed allowances. Add the value of benefits in kind (housing provided, school paid, annual flight divided by 12). Add end of service, about 0.7 months of base per year divided by 12. Leave the bonus aside.

### 2. Subtract your real fixed costs

List what you will pay yourself: rent (and the housing fee of 5% of annual rent billed with electricity), schools, family health insurance if the employer does not cover it, car or transport. Our guide to the [cost of living in Dubai](/en/cost-of-living-dubai/) gives orders of magnitude by profile.

### 3. Compare with your current net

Take your current net salary, after tax and contributions, and subtract the same items (rent, schools, insurance). You are then comparing what you really have left in both countries, which is the only honest comparison.

### 4. Check your tax situation

Not paying tax in the UAE does not mean no longer paying any at all. If your spouse, your children or most of your interests remain in your country, it may continue to tax you. Settle this question before signing.

### 5. Look beyond the first year

A job that sponsors your family, pays for school and guarantees a decent end of service is often worth more than a higher salary with nothing around it. Think about stability too: your visa depends on your job, unless you obtain a Green or Golden Visa.

## What can really be negotiated

- **The share of base salary** in the total, for end of service.
- **The housing allowance**, to compare with rents in the area where you want to live.
- **Family health insurance** and its level of cover (maternity, dental, care in your home country): see our article on [health insurance in Dubai](/en/blog/health-insurance-dubai/).
- **School fees**, in full or in part.
- **The annual flight** for the whole family.
- **The start date and relocation costs** (removal, hotel for the first weeks).
- **The notice period**, which ranges from 30 to 90 days depending on the contract.

Recruitment and visa costs are the employer's responsibility: they cannot be negotiated and must never be billed to you.

## Checklist before signing

1. The base salary and each allowance are written separately, in figures.
2. You know who pays for your family's health insurance, and for what cover.
3. You know the end-of-service system (standard, savings plan or DIFC).
4. Your salary reaches the thresholds you need (family, Green or Golden Visa).
5. You have calculated your disposable income after rent, schools and insurance.
6. Your tax residence in your home country is clarified.
7. No recruitment or visa fee is asked of you.
